## 1. Prerequisite bug found: `action` is not comparable to `q`

The first run produced nonsense — lags pinned to the ±600 ms search rails, negative
correlations, a *negative* end-to-end lag. Cause:

`state_logger` writes the two signals in **different spaces and different joint orders**:

| CSV | space | order |
|---|---|---|
| `q.csv` | radians, `default_angles` **already added back** | hardware / MuJoCo |
| `action.csv` | **raw policy output, unscaled** | IsaacLab |

The deploy binary reconstructs the target as
`q_target[i] = default_angles[i] + action[isaaclab_to_mujoco[i]] * g1_action_scale[i]`.

| comparison | median \|Δ\| | median corr |
|---|---|---|
| raw `action` vs `q` | 0.667 rad (38°) | **−0.015** |
| reconstructed `q_target` vs `q` | **0.056 rad** | **0.912** |

Implemented in `data_process/g1_params.py`, with constants **generated by compiling against
`policy_parameters.hpp`** rather than transcribed. Note `g1_action_scale` spans 0.0745
(wrist roll/pitch) → 0.548 (hip/waist yaw), a ~7× range, so this is not a small correction.

## 2. ✅ The robot-side logs are exactly self-consistent

Testing whether the PD law holds **at zero lag**:

```
tau  ?=  Kp * (q_target - q)  -  Kd * dq
```

| metric | result |
|---|---|
| correlation | **1.000** on **29/29** joints |
| regression slope | 0.984 – 0.996 |

This is a strong validation with two consequences:

1. The `q_target` reconstruction is **exactly right** (a wrong scale or joint permutation
   could not produce corr = 1.000 on every joint).
2. `q_target`, `q`, `dq` and `motor_torque` **on the same CSV row are the values the
   controller actually used**. There is **no logging skew and no computational latency**
   between command and measurement in the log.

## 3. ❌ A1 — actuator lag is NOT measurable by cross-correlation here

Cross-correlating `q_target[j] → q[j]` (high-passed, ±400 ms bound) gives a consistent but
**physically impossible** answer:

| group | n | median lag | median corr |
|---|---|---|---|
| legs | 10 | −40.6 ms | 0.379 |
| left arm | 5 | −41.6 ms | 0.589 |
| right arm | 5 | −56.7 ms | 0.456 |
| **overall** | **20/29** | **−42.1 ms (−2.11 steps)** | — |

Negative lag means *q leads its own command* — impossible for a causal actuator.

**Cause: closed-loop feedback.** The policy computes `action(t)` **from** `q(t)`, so the
log contains both causal paths at once:

```
q --(policy feedback, ~1 step)--> action        <-- dominates the correlation
action --(actuator dynamics)--> q
```

Since §2 proved there is no logging skew, the measured −42 ms is the **feedback** path, not
actuator response. Cross-correlation cannot separate the two directions in closed loop.

**Actuator lag therefore cannot be obtained this way** — it needs the mechanical response
(torque → acceleration), which is exactly Phase B.

## 4. ❌ A2 — end-to-end latency is not measurable from these logs

Human upper-body motion energy vs robot arm motion energy:

| metric | value |
|---|---|
| whole-window lag | −89.8 ms (corr 0.379) |
| per-20 s chunks (16 usable) | median **−164 ms**, mean −217 ms, **std 204 ms** |
| p10 / p90 | −375 ms / −80 ms |

Again **negative** — the robot appears to move *before* the human.

**Cause: the two sides use different machines' clocks.** PICO `timestamp_realtime` comes from
the VR host PC; robot `time_realtime_ms` from the robot's onboard computer (independently
confirmed earlier: monotonic clocks are ~948,000–1,376,000 s vs ~2,000–2,800 s, i.e. different
uptimes). NTP keeps them within seconds, but the **sub-second offset is unknown and
uncalibrated** — and it is exactly the quantity we are trying to measure.

The measured value = true latency + unknown constant clock offset. **The mean is not
trustworthy.** The spread (±204 ms) also carries measurement noise (median chunk corr 0.479),
so it is an upper bound on jitter, not a clean estimate.

## 5. ✅ What IS measurable: steady-state tracking error

`q_target − q` is well-defined regardless of the lag confound, and is a direct measure of
how far the real actuator falls short of its command:

| group | RMS | p95 | mean \|τ\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| legs | **13.19°** | 28.53° | 7.73 N·m |
| waist | **13.55°** | 34.04° | 3.65 N·m |
| left arm | 7.37° | 16.51° | 1.08 N·m |
| right arm | 8.10° | 17.56° | 1.23 N·m |
| **overall** | **10.95°** (0.191 rad) | — | — |

Worst joints:

| joint | RMS error | mean \|τ\| |
|---|---|---|
| `L_ankle_pitch` | **28.72°** | 11.52 N·m |
| `R_ankle_pitch` | **26.69°** | 11.72 N·m |
| `waist_pitch` | 22.72° | 8.10 N·m |
| `R_sho_pitch` | 13.67° | 2.53 N·m |

**Observations**

- A ~11° mean tracking deficit is large. With `Kp ≈ 28.5` on the ankles, 28.7° of error is
  ~14 N·m of commanded torque — the joints are working hard and still not arriving.
- **Ankle pitch and waist pitch are the worst**, and they carry the **highest torques**. This
  is the classic signature of gravity load + unmodelled friction/saturation in the
  weight-bearing chain — precisely what Tan et al. and Hwangbo et al. model explicitly.
- Arms track ~1.7× better than legs at ~1/7 the torque, consistent with the load hypothesis
  rather than a control-tuning problem.
- This is measured on the **real** robot only; whether sim shows the same deficit is Phase C.

## 6. Conclusions and consequences for the plan

1. **Robot logs are trustworthy and internally exact** (PD law recovers at corr 1.000). This
   is the green light for Phase B — the signals needed for actuator ID are sound.
2. **Latency cannot be recovered from these logs.** Not a data-processing failure: A1 is
   blocked by closed-loop feedback, A2 by an uncalibrated inter-machine clock offset.
3. **The tracking-error result is the usable Phase A output**, and it already points at the
   likely dominant gap: high-load pitch joints (ankles, waist).

### To actually measure latency, one of these is required

- **Instrument the deploy binary** to log, per frame, the PICO `timestamp_realtime` of the
  SMPL sample it consumed. Then latency = `robot_time − consumed_sample_time` directly, on a
  single clock. *(cheapest and most reliable)*
- **A shared hardware event** visible to both machines (a sync pulse / clap) to calibrate the
  constant clock offset.
- **Open-loop step tests** on hardware (command a step, record response) to isolate actuator
  lag without the feedback confound.

### Revised next step

Proceed to **Phase B (actuator identification)**, which is unblocked and now better motivated:
§2 gives an exact PD baseline and §5 localises the deficit to high-torque pitch joints. The
residual `tau_measured − Kp·e + Kd·dq` is ~0 by construction, so Phase B must instead model
**q's mechanical response to tau** (inertia, friction, saturation) — the quantity A1 could not
reach.

## 7. Latency decomposition — what each stage contributes

§4 showed the *cross-machine* leg is unmeasurable. Everything **inside the robot** shares one
clock, so those stages **are** measurable. Pipeline:

```
PICO capture ──①──> ZMQ ──②──> encoder ──③──> policy ──③──> motor cmd ──④──> motion
             (cross-machine)   (─────── same control step ───────)
```

| # | Stage | Measurable? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| ① | PICO capture → ZMQ arrival | ❌ | different machines' clocks; offset unknown (§4) |
| ② | ZMQ arrival → policy consumption | ✅ **indirectly** | **median 20 ms, p95 140 ms** (staleness) |
| ③ | encoder → policy → motor command | ✅ | **< 20 ms — same control step** |
| ④ | motor command → joint motion | ⚠️ | blocked by feedback confound (§3) → Phase B |

### Stage ③ — encoder + policy + motor command: **within one control step**

The PD identity `tau = Kp(q_target − q) − Kd·dq` holds at **corr = 1.000, zero lag, 29/29
joints** (§2). Tokens, action, and the resulting torque on a given row are all mutually
consistent, so the entire compute chain completes inside one 20 ms tick. **This stage is not
a source of gap.**

Control loop timing is also excellent:

| metric | value |
|---|---|
| period | median **20.00 ms** (50.0 Hz) |
| jitter | **std 0.11 ms**, p99 20.16 ms, max 25.8 ms |

### Stage ② — SMPL delivery: the real finding

`token_state` is the encoded SMPL input. When consecutive rows are **bit-identical**, the
policy re-consumed the same human frame — i.e. no new SMPL arrived that tick.

| metric | all | excluding dropouts ≥0.5 s |
|---|---|---|
| stale frames | 39.4 % | **36.1 %** |
| effective fresh-SMPL rate | 30.3 Hz | **32.0 Hz** |
| median stale run | 20 ms | **20 ms** |
| p95 stale run | 155 ms | **140 ms** |

**The human stream is captured at 70.4 Hz but reaches the policy at only ~32 Hz.** Since
70 Hz > 50 Hz control, *every* tick should have had a fresh frame. Roughly **one control step
in three runs on a stale human command**, with a p95 staleness of 140 ms — comparable to the
`STREAMING_DATA_ABSENT_THRESHOLD` of 150 ms.

Separately, **22.6 s (5.2 %) of the window is in dropouts ≥ 0.5 s**, including one **18.2 s**
outage at t≈198 s:

| t (s) | duration |
|---|---|
| 198.0 | **18.24 s** |
| 194.8 | 0.90 s |
| 196.1 | 0.82 s |
| 134.2 / 170.9 / 326.8 | 0.68 s each |

During these the policy holds the last human pose while the robot keeps being commanded.

> ⚠️ **Consequence for Phase C.** Sim replay feeds SMPL at a clean 50 Hz with no staleness. The
> real robot ran on a ~32 Hz effective input with 140 ms p95 staleness and a 18 s dropout. Any
> sim-vs-real difference will partly be *this*, not dynamics. Either reproduce the measured
> staleness pattern in sim, or exclude the dropout windows — otherwise the "dynamics gap" is
> contaminated.

### Why the deploy binary's own timings could not be used

`g1_deploy_onnx_ref.cpp:4082` already prints `LowState age`, `Streaming data mean/std delay`,
`Obs`, `Policy`, `Obs 2 Motor Command` every 50 ticks — exactly this decomposition. But they go
to **stdout only, never to CSV**, and no stdout capture from the Aug 11 run exists on disk.

Note also `GetLastUpdateTime()` (`zmq_endpoint_interface.hpp:578`) returns the true PICO
capture timestamp **only when `is_localhost_`**; otherwise it falls back to `last_receive_time_`
(local arrival). The Aug 11 run used separate machines, so even that counter would have
measured arrival, not end-to-end.

**Recommended one-line fix:** add `streaming_data_delay` and the obs/policy durations to the
`state_logger` CSV set, and log the consumed sample's PICO `timestamp_realtime`. That makes
stage ① and ② directly measurable on future runs.

## 8. Decomposition v2 — using the policy-side recording `streamed_081851`

`/home/grease/g1_robot_data/streamed_081851/` is the deploy binary's own recording of the SMPL
it **received**. Comparing it against the PICO capture measures stage ①+② **by frame
correspondence, with no clocks involved** — which sidesteps §4's cross-machine problem entirely.

### ⚠️ Two corrections to earlier conclusions

**(a) `streamed_081851` is 50 Hz, not 70 Hz.**

| hypothesis | correlation |
|---|---|
| **50 Hz, aligned to Mode-2 start** | **0.9970** ✅ |
| 70 Hz @ 164.6 s (earlier claim) | 0.047 ❌ |

Independently confirmed by frame count: 24,453 received vs 489.1 s × 50 Hz = 24,455 expected.
It is written **once per control step** and spans **exactly** the Mode-2 window. The earlier
70 Hz figure came from a rate-scale search that found a spurious optimum; the note in
`PICO_SMPL_STREAMING_DATASETS_NOTE.md` §"streamed_081851 ⊂ smpl_raw_real_robot" should be
read with this correction.

**(b) The PICO capture is ~46-50 Hz, not 70.4 Hz. Its clock FREEZES.**

| | |
|---|---|
| duplicate timestamps | **52.7 %** of frames |
| unique timestamps | 24,313 / 51,438 |
| **worst stuck group** | **17,786 frames sharing ONE timestamp** |
| stuck block | frames **33,652 – 51,437** (final 34.6 % of capture) |
| location | **inside the Mode-2 window** |
| true rate (valid region) | **46.06 Hz** (33,652 frames / 730.6 s) |

The "70.4 Hz" used earlier was `frames / span` computed *with* the frozen tail included, so it
overcounted. Per-60 s buckets consistently show ~50 Hz, matching the corrected figure.

> **`timestamp_realtime` is unusable for the last third of this capture.** Use `frame_index`
> (verified monotonic and unique) or content matching instead.

### Result: transport is essentially 1:1, with ~11 % loss

Content matching is unaffected by the clock problem — **100 % of received frames matched a
PICO frame exactly** (median error 5.0e-10, i.e. bit-identical float32).

| metric | value |
|---|---|
| PICO frames spanned | 11,705 – 33,656 (21,952) |
| distinct frames delivered | 19,580 |
| **delivered** | **89.2 %** |
| **dropped** | **10.8 %** |

Per-control-step advance through the PICO stream (capture ≈50 Hz, control 50 Hz → expect 1.0):

| advance | share | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **19.9 %** | stale — no new frame this step |
| 1 | **75.9 %** | nominal 1:1 |
| 2 | 3.7 % | catch-up after a drop |
| ≥3 | 0.5 % | catch-up after a longer gap |
| **mean** | **0.850** | pipeline tracks ~1:1 |

**This revises §7's headline.** There I inferred "captured at 70.4 Hz but reaching the policy at
only ~32 Hz", implying the pipeline discards a third of the input. With the corrected capture
rate that framing is wrong: capture and control are **both ~50 Hz**, and the pipeline runs
**~1:1**. The genuine losses are:

- **10.8 % of frames dropped outright**, and
- **~20 % of control steps running on a repeated frame** (§7's `token_state` staleness measure,
  36 %, is a *different* and stricter quantity: it also counts steps where the encoder re-ran on
  an unchanged buffer).

### Implication for Phase C (unchanged, but better quantified)

Sim replay feeds a clean 50 Hz stream with no drops. The real run had **10.8 % dropped frames**
and **19.9 % repeated-input steps**. To attribute sim-vs-real differences to *dynamics*, either
replay the exact delivered sequence (the `best[]` index mapping reconstructs it) or restrict the
comparison to clean stretches.
